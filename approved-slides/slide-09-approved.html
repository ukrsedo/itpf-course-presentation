Software Inflation Drivers

First distinguish the five direct drivers of software cost growth:

1

Price inflation

The unit price rises while licence scope and quantity stay unchanged.

Example: Salesforce announced an average 9% list-price increase in 2023.
2

Volume inflation

The billable population, seats, licences or service units increase.

Example: Oracle Java SE Universal Subscription uses an employee-based metric.
3

Add-on inflation

Premium modules, AI features or support tiers are added to the baseline product.

Example: Microsoft 365 Copilot requires a qualifying Microsoft 365 subscription.
4

Portfolio inflation

New applications enter the estate, often creating overlap or redundancy.

Example: Microsoft 365 expands with Power BI, Visio, Project and Viva.
5

Consumption inflation

Usage-based charges rise through tokens, APIs, storage, compute or transactions.

Example: Azure OpenAI charges pay-as-you-go input and output tokens.

Three further effects can increase cost or distort how inflation is measured:

6

SaaS shrinkflation

Baseline prices rise while feature access is reduced or existing tiers are split, so customers may need a higher plan or separate licence for the same utility. Example: in 2024 Microsoft introduced new commercial Microsoft 365 and Office 365 suites without Teams and a standalone Teams offer.
7

Statistical discrepancy

Economic indices such as CPI or PCE use defined product baskets and quality-adjustment methods that may not match an organisation’s SaaS portfolio. Example: a Microsoft 365 renewal can combine seat changes, bundle changes and Copilot add-ons that a broad software index cannot isolate as one like-for-like price movement.
8

Category-specific inflation

Price growth is uneven across software categories, editions and demand segments. Example: Salesforce’s 2025 update targeted Enterprise and Unlimited editions of selected Sales, Service and Field Service products, while Starter and Pro editions were unchanged.
