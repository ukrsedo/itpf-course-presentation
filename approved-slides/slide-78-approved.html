Session 5 Source-to-Contract

Incomplete Contracts and the Hold-up Problem

Why the risk appears

A buyer makes a relationship-specific investment because cooperation is expected to create additional profit.

Once the investment is sunk, switching becomes more expensive. The supplier may then have an incentive to exploit the buyer’s new dependency.
1
Specific investmentThe buyer invests in equipment or capability with limited value outside the relationship.
2
DependencyThe investment changes the buyer’s switching economics after commitment.
3
Opportunistic repricingThe supplier attempts to capture more value after the buyer is locked in.
4
Value destructionIf the buyer exits, the buyer strands investment and the supplier loses future business.

How procurement can reduce the risk

Vertical integration / insourcingOwn the critical capability where dependency on one supplier is unacceptable.
Long-term contractual protectionFix critical pricing rules, change mechanisms, decision rights and exit protections before the investment is made.
Revenue or risk sharingAlign incentives so both parties benefit from preserving the cooperative surplus.
Relational governanceUse transparency, escalation and joint decision-making where every future state cannot be written into the contract.
The key risk is not merely a transfer of value through a higher price. Opportunism can trigger an exit decision that destroys value for both parties.

Game assumptions

Annual business volume£1.0m
Remaining commercial horizon3 years
PBuyer profit increase from investment£150k / yr
VBuyer specialised investment£180k once
RSupplier opportunistic price increase£220k / yr
BSupplier annual business margin at risk£150k / yr

For the exercise, B assumes a 15% supplier contribution margin on the £1.0m annual business volume. Payoffs below are incremental to the original contract economics.

After the buyer invests, what should each party do — and what happens to value if the supplier exploits the lock-in?
Stage 1 — Buyer invests V = £180k. The investment can create P = £150k additional profit per year for the remaining 3-year relationship.

Scenario 1 · Supplier keeps agreed pricing

Cooperation continues for all three years.

Buyer = 3P − V = 3×150 − 180
Buyer: +£270k
Supplier: baseline economics retained

Stage 2 · Supplier raises price by R = £220k/year

The increase comes only after the buyer’s specialised investment is sunk.

Buyer must now compare staying for 3 years with switching after Year 1.
Stage 3 — Buyer response to the opportunistic increase

Scenario 2 · Accept increase and stay

Buyer = 3(P − R) − V
= 3×(150 − 220) − 180 = −£390k
Supplier incremental gain = 3R = +£660k
Short-term value is transferred to the supplier, but the buyer’s three-year economics become negative.

Scenario 3 · Absorb Year 1, then switch

Buyer = P − V − R
= 150 − 180 − 220 = −£250k
Supplier = R − 2B
= 220 − 300 = −£80k
Switching is less damaging to the buyer than staying (−£250k vs −£390k), but it strands the investment and costs the supplier two years of business margin.
Game-theory lesson: cooperation gives the buyer +£270k. Opportunistic repricing makes staying economically irrational; the rational response is to switch even though switching still produces a loss. The result is a destructive branch where buyer −£250k and supplier −£80k. Opportunism therefore destroys the cooperative surplus that both parties could have preserved.
